Why not?

Why aren’t the best creativity books written for product designers?

I recently started writing a short post recommending my favourite books on creativity. Halfway through, something struck me.

They’re all written by people from advertising or marketing.

I’m not aware of many books about creativity aimed at product designers, or people working inside digital product teams. Why is that?

One possibility is simple ignorance. Maybe they exist and I’ve missed them. But I suspect something else is going on.

Product design doesn’t reward creativity in quite the same way marketing does.

In advertising, radically different ideas are the goal. Surprise, disruption, emotional impact. In product design, especially digital products, those same qualities can feel risky. When someone is trying to get a job done, novelty often looks like friction. Familiar patterns reduce learning, reduce error, and increase speed. From that perspective, creative interfaces aren’t always better interfaces.

Then there are technical realities. Bold ideas tend to mean new components, new logic, new edge cases, and less compatibility with what already exists. Even when engineering teams are excited by the challenge, creative deviation brings more uncertainty, longer timelines, and higher delivery risk. Pragmatism usually wins.

So big software projects often start with ambitious creative visions, then gradually retreat toward familiar, proven patterns. Not because teams lack imagination, but because certainty compounds value.

And to be clear, that isn’t a failure. Product design isn’t advertising. Success isn’t measured by memorability alone.

But that doesn’t mean creativity has no place.

The best products don’t just work. They feel human. They give pleasure. They’re relatable. And humans, inconveniently, are not logical creatures.

As Matias Duarte (Google’s VP of design) put it:

"In a business environment we often anchor on the things that are easiest to measure, like usability. But it’s important to explore the things that are harder to quantify. Because it could turn out that feelings are the most impactful.”

Designers are often taught to justify every decision. Always ask why. That’s useful, but it can also lead to solutions that are impeccably rational and quietly uninspiring.

The books I keep returning to don’t argue against constraints. They challenge which constraints are fixed. They encourage bending them, reframing them, or occasionally discarding them altogether.

So maybe the question for product designers isn’t always why.

Sometimes it should be: why not?.

The books by the way:

  • It's not how good you are, it's how good you want to be. Paul Arden
  • Whatever you think, think the opposite. Paul Arden
  • John Hegarty on Creativity, there are no rules. John Hegarty
  • Alchemy. Rory Sutherland

I got rather distracted from the original post of discussing why I am recommending these books. I'll return to this soon!

  • You’re a creative

    Whether you’re an artist, a carpenter, a teacher or a dentist, creativity shows up in most jobs, even if it isn’t always obvious.

  • Where Does Creativity Fit?

    You can follow a collection of frameworks, workshops and methodologies to ensure your designs are really well validated and meet all the project requirements. Does it matter if creativity is largely neglected in the process?

  • Making uncomfortable comfortable

    Great design often emerges gradually. It’s shaped through iteration, learning and a willingness to sit with a bit of uncertainty along the way.

  • Alchemy

    Ostensibly written about the value of divergent thinking in marketing - but I really can't recommend this book enough for product designers.

  • Usability, utility and craft

    A look at Google Maps and the application of craft, usability and utility in it's design over the last 20 years.

  • The making of

    An overview of how I went about building this website, the process of designing in the browser and making some pragmatic choices to just get it built and published.

  • The case for working slow

    Whatever field you work in there’s likely a push towards efficiency and increasing productivity. Here's an alternative take on how the avoidance of certain efficiencies is in itself an efficiency.

  • Did somebody say dark patterns?

    Dark, or deceptive, UX patterns are designs that force the user to take an action that is not in their best interest. They can also be highly effective at boosting conversions.

  • ChatGPT and me

    A few observations on using AI to build coded prototypes as part of the design process.

  • Sustainabe Design for digital transformation

    Digital transformation isn’t just about technology. It drives cultural and mindset shifts that shape our thoughts, actions, and behaviours.

  • Climate Action & the Expanding Role of Designers

    Design won’t solve climate change on its own. But neither will digital transformation be sustainable without designers willing to challenge assumptions

  • Digital Sustainability, Biodiversity & Designing Within Ecosystems

    I’m increasingly seeing Human-Centred Design as needing to expand into something broader — designing with people, planet, and systems in mind.

  • Designing sustainable systems

    Digital transformation can accelerate climate action, biodiversity protection, and pollution reduction — but only if it’s intentionally designed.

    A short reflection on my key takeaways after completing the UN's Digital4Sustainability course.
