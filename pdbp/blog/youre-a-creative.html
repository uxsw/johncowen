You’re a creative

Whether you’re an artist, a carpenter, a teacher or a dentist, creativity shows up in most jobs, even if it isn’t always obvious.

In some roles it’s right there on the surface. In others, it’s much quieter. It might show up in how you approach a tricky problem, how you adapt when circumstances change, or how you make decisions within a set of constraints. Creative dentistry or creative accounting aren’t phrases you hear very often, but the best people in those professions tend to think a little more broadly than the job description alone might suggest.

It’s perfectly possible to have a successful career by following established standards, applying best practice and delivering exactly what’s expected. In many settings, particularly regulated or high-risk ones, that level of rigour really matters. But over time, I’ve noticed that the people who stand out tend to do something slightly different. They’re curious about whether the “right” answer is still the right one.

Very few solutions are ever truly finished. Technology moves on, we learn more, and the world around us keeps changing. What worked well a few years ago might still function perfectly, but that doesn’t always mean it couldn’t be better. Just because innovation isn’t required doesn’t mean it isn’t possible, or worth exploring.

If you’re aiming for something that’s good enough, then sticking closely to the rulebook is often the safest approach. You’ll deliver what people were expecting, and most of the time everyone will be happy with the result. But if you’re aiming for something genuinely better, something that feels like progress rather than polish, then you usually have to be willing to push a little further.

That can be uncomfortable. New ideas tend to feel risky at first. They ask people to rethink familiar habits, and they don’t always land immediately. That doesn’t make them wrong, just unfamiliar.

I think many creative people carry a mild sense of restlessness. Not in a disruptive or contrarian way, but a quiet sense that things can probably be improved. When presented with a very logical, well-reasoned solution, there’s often an instinct to ask what’s missing, or what assumptions it’s built on. Logic is useful, but it’s also rooted in what’s already known and proven. On its own, it often leads to incremental change rather than anything truly new.

There are plenty of situations where that’s exactly what’s needed. Some organisations are deliberately risk-averse, and there’s real value in making small, careful improvements to established products and services. But for people who enjoy creative work, those projects can sometimes feel a little flat.

The projects that tend to be most rewarding are the ones where there’s room to explore. Where clients are open to the idea that meaningful change might involve trying something that hasn’t been done before, and accepting a bit of uncertainty along the way. The ideas that come out of those spaces don’t always look sensible at first glance. They can feel illogical, or at least hard to justify using existing patterns and frameworks.

This is something I’ve seen play out a lot in large design programmes, particularly those grounded in human-centred design.

HCD gives us some very solid foundations. Research, testing and validation help ensure that what we design is usable, accessible and inclusive. Those things really matter, and in many contexts they’re non-negotiable. Research gives us confidence that a design works.

But people aren’t always as rational as our frameworks assume. We’re influenced by emotion, familiarity and instinct, often in ways that are hard to measure. It’s possible to design something that performs well against every metric and still feels a bit flat. It works, but it doesn’t quite spark anything.

I think this is where creative intent can sometimes get lost. Not because teams don’t care, or lack skill, but because it’s harder to defend an idea that’s driven by intuition as well as evidence. Over time, design can drift towards being about delivery and validation, rather than exploration and authorship.

For me, this is where more senior designers can make a real difference.

Leadership in design isn’t about rejecting standards, research or regulation. It’s about working comfortably within them, while also recognising their limits. It’s about knowing when optimisation is enough, and when it might be worth exploring something more ambitious. Often that means helping teams and stakeholders feel safe taking small, thoughtful risks.

It’s also about sharing that thinking with others. Helping less experienced designers see that creativity isn’t at odds with rigour, and that working within constraints doesn’t mean switching your imagination off. In many cases, the constraints are what make the creative work interesting in the first place.

I don’t think creativity is something you either have or don’t. It’s more of a habit: a willingness to question, to stay curious, and to be slightly dissatisfied with “done”. The aim isn’t to ignore best practice or tear up the rulebook. It’s simply to recognise that the rulebook was written at a particular moment in time.

Good designers apply what’s known to work. Great designers also keep an eye on what might work next. Knowing when to do each is, I think, a big part of what design leadership looks like.
